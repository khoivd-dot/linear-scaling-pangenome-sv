## Summary of the gap

We found no published study that measures how much a chunked PGGB-style build (separate graphs per window, then stitched) differs from a monolithic build of the same region. The closest prior work comes in three forms. nf-core/pangenome [8] distributes the all-to-all wfmash alignment step across cluster nodes, but it then induces one graph from the combined alignments, so it is chunked alignment, not chunked graphs. The impg README [14] documents `partition` and `lace` and says plainly that it makes no accuracy comparison against a whole-graph build, that partitioning "does not cap peak memory", and that users should measure on their own data. Large resources such as HPRC release 1 [12] and Minigraph-Cactus [10] build graphs per chromosome (or per clipped chromosome-scale component), which is a coarse partition at natural boundaries, but we did not locate a paper that benchmarks fine-grained cutting against an uncut build.

So the following look open as far as this search reaches: (a) a controlled accuracy comparison (truvari against dipcall or T2T truth, stratified by GIAB context) of chunked versus monolithic graphs; (b) the finding that cut position (recombination hotspot versus fixed or random) does not matter; (c) the CPU and memory break-even near 40 haplotypes on 1 Mb; (d) the segmental-duplication failure mode where paralogous copies fall into different chunks; (e) the seqwish `-B 10M` pass-through problem in PGGB v0.6.0. These claims should be worded as "not located in the sources searched on 2026-11-24", not as "no prior work exists". The impg manuscript ("Implicit pangenome graphs", cited only through a draft cover letter seen in a search snippet) may contain its own validation of partition and lace; we could not read it, so check it before claiming priority on the stitching result. We also could not find any page for haploblocks.org, so nothing here establishes prior art for haplotype-block-based pangenome partitioning.

## Related work by topic

**PGGB and its components.** PGGB builds reference-free graphs in three stages: all-to-all alignment with wfmash, graph induction with seqwish, and smoothing with smoothxg, followed by gfaffix normalisation and odgi-based statistics [1,2]. The Nature Methods paper is the primary citation for the pipeline [1]; the bioRxiv preprint is its earlier version [2]. Our work treats PGGB as the unit being chunked. wfmash has no stand-alone paper; its README points to the wavefront aligner (Marco-Sola et al. 2020, 2023), the MashMap2 homology-mapping line of work (Jain et al. 2018) and the minmer paper (Kille et al. 2023), and gives a Zenodo software DOI [3]. seqwish [4] computes the transitive closure of aligned bases to induce the graph; the `-B` batch parameter we report as under-collapsing lives here. odgi [5] supplies the graph operations (sort, unchop, view) used after stitching. smoothxg has no stand-alone paper that we found and is described in [1]. gfaffix has no paper either; its README carries no citation [7], and the HPRC paper [12] is the usual reference in downstream documents (that attribution comes from a secondary source and is UNVERIFIED). The nf-core/pangenome workflow [8] wraps the same stages and parallelises alignment across a cluster.

**Other graph builders.** minigraph [9] builds a reference-anchored structural-variant graph quickly from assemblies, and Minigraph-Cactus [10] turns minigraph alignments into a base-level graph using a modified Cactus base aligner and vg tooling, scaling to 90 HPRC haplotypes. Progressive Cactus [11] aligns genomes along a guide tree and is built around decomposing the problem into subproblems, which is conceptually the closest established precedent for divide-and-conquer alignment; it is a multiple-genome aligner, not a PGGB-type graph. We did not verify any statement in [11] about how subproblem boundaries affect accuracy.

**HPRC.** The HPRC draft pangenome [12] reports 47 phased diploid assemblies and is the data and benchmark context for [10]. Release 2 has a preprint titled "HPRC2: A human pangenome reference with near-complete coverage of common genetic variation" [13], reporting 460 haplotypes from 232 individuals; we saw its title, DOI and abstract-level claims only in a search listing, so authors, server and date need checking. We use release 2 assemblies.

**Partition, stitch and chunking.** impg [14] indexes pairwise alignments (PAF) with implicit interval trees, extracts homologous loci, partitions a cohort into windows (`partition`), can build each window's graph with pggb, seqwish or poa, and merges per-window GFA or VCF files (`lace`), optionally filling gaps with reference sequence. This is the same stitch operation we evaluate, and its documentation is explicit that it offers no accuracy comparison [14]. The nf-core/pangenome paper [8] shows near-linear runtime reduction when alignment jobs are split into chunks and a 2 to 3 times speedup over PGGB on 1000 chr19 haplotypes; its accuracy treatment could not be read here. Adjacent but different designs: PGR-TK decomposes a pangenome into principal bundles rather than building by chunks [34]; MumemtoM partitions inputs to compute multi-MUMs and merges them [33]. Both are UNVERIFIED beyond snippets.

**Graph tooling and evaluation.** The vg toolkit [15] and its structural-variant genotyping extension [16] define the graph model and `vg deconstruct`, which we use to project graphs to VCF; Giraffe [17] is the mapper built on the same framework. truvari [18] compares structural-variant calls with allele-aware matching. dipcall comes from the synthetic-diploid benchmark paper [19] (that the dipcall caller is introduced there is from our recollection of the paper; the search snippet did not mention it, so treat as UNVERIFIED). The GIAB stratifications [20] partition the genome into contexts (including segmental duplications and low-mappability regions) so that accuracy can be reported per context, and the Q100 HG002 complete diploid benchmark [21] extends high-confidence regions into previously excluded difficult sequence.

**Recombination maps and haplotype blocks.** The HapMap phase II map [22], the deCODE sequence-level map [23] and the pyrho population-specific maps [24] are the three maps against which hotspot-based cut positions can be checked. Hotspots as block boundaries is an old idea in population genetics, but we found no source for haploblocks.org or for haplotype-block-based pangenome partitioning [25]. A search turned up only generic haplotype-block literature, so the null result on hotspot cuts has no direct prior comparison that we could locate.

**Segmental duplications and structural haplotypes.** Vollger et al. [26,27] characterise SDs in T2T-CHM13 and in HPRC assemblies, including elevated SNV density and interlocus gene conversion inside SDs. Both results explain why paralogous copies are hard to place in one graph and why alignments in SDs are ambiguous. The 17q21.31 (MAPT) inversion region has been characterised by Zody et al. [28] and by two companion papers on its structural diversity (Steinberg et al. [29]; Boettger et al. [30]). For 22q11.2, Pastor et al. [31] used optical mapping to resolve LCR22 haplotypes and Vervoort et al. [32] used long reads and fibre-FISH to show multiple paralogues and recombination mechanisms. The LCR22 literature reports high variability in size and structure of the region, and reference gaps inside LCR22A, which supports our observation that chunk placement matters most there.

**PanSN.** The PanSN-spec naming convention (sample#haplotype#contig) is used throughout PGGB, impg and HPRC files [35]; we could not open its page, so cite the repository at github.com/pangenome/PanSN-spec only after checking it.
